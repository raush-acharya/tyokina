# TyoKina case study: 15-minute presenter script

Planned running time **14:17** across **39 slides**. Press **P** on the site for presenter mode (notes, timer, pace against these timestamps), **G** for the overview.

`[step]` marks a key press that reveals the next beat on the same slide. Spoken pace is about 140 words a minute; the word count per slide is a check on that.

## Brief

### 01 · Cover  `00:00` · 17s · 35 words

Hi, I'm Raush. This is TyoKina, a product-research app I designed in two intense weeks, solo. I'll show you the process, not just the screens: what I did, what I found, and what I'd change.

### 02 · The name  `00:17` · 15s · 34 words

First, the name. In Nepali, "tyo" means "that", and "kina" means both "why" and "to buy". Buy that, why that? Most tools only help with the first question. TyoKina is built around the second.

### 03 · Buying one thing shouldn’t take twenty tabs  `00:32` · 31s · 68 words

Here's the problem. Before buying headphones or a laptop, people hop between marketplaces, review sites, YouTube and Reddit, and still aren't sure who to trust. I split the frustration into six things: it takes too long, it's hard to trust, it's overwhelming, you regret the price, there's no single place for it, and nowhere honest for owners to speak. Every later decision traces back to one of these.

### 04 · So I gave myself a project: do the whole process, once, properly  `01:03` · 28s · 68 words

Why this project? I wanted to learn product design by doing every stage, from an unclear problem to a usable direction. Two weeks, solo, my first full project. And I'll be upfront: I haven't run user interviews yet. The insights come from desk research and competitor analysis, and every persona is an assumption. Honesty about evidence is the product's first principle, so it had to be mine too.

### 05 · I followed the whole design process, step by step  `01:31` · 19s · 46 words

I followed the classic design-thinking path: five stages, in order, looping back whenever a later step exposed a gap. Empathize, define, ideate, prototype, test. Notice the last circle is dashed. Testing is designed but not yet run, and I'd rather show you that honestly than pretend.

## Empathize

### 06 · Empathize  `01:50` · 3s · 3 words

Stage one. Empathize.

### 07 · What I did, and what I haven’t  `01:53` · 25s · 60 words

Here's what I actually did. Secondary research: articles, forum threads, how review sites work. A competitive analysis of four platform types. And six proto-personas, merged into three. What I haven't done is primary research. I wrote an eighteen-question interview guide, ready to run, but it hasn't been run. The question behind it all: why does buying research feel so exhausting?

### 08 · Everyone solves a slice of the problem  `02:18` · 21s · 50 words

The competitive analysis was clear. AI assistants are instant, but can invent facts. Expert sites bring lab testing, but update slowly and miss local products. Communities have real owners, but are contradictory. Marketplaces have convenience, but sponsored listings. Each solves one slice, and leaves a gap the buyer fills alone.

### 09 · Five things the research kept telling me  `02:39` · 19s · 46 words

Five findings kept repeating. AI starts the journey, it doesn't finish it. The real journey is a relay across tools. People want confidence, not more data. Fake reviews have entered a new era. And shopping is going agentic. Click the board to see it in Figma.

### 10 · The buyer ends up being the glue  `02:58` · 25s · 60 words

Put those together and you get the insight that drove everything. Every platform solves one part. None solves the whole journey. Ask AI, Google it, check Reddit, watch YouTube, compare prices, then buy and hope. Today, the buyer is the glue, carrying context from tool to tool in their head. That's the job I wanted to take off their shoulders.

## Define

### 11 · Define  `03:23` · 3s · 3 words

Stage two. Define.

### 12 · Four reasons buying feels so tiring  `03:26` · 15s · 34 words

I grouped the research into four themes: research fatigue, distrust of commercial content, a trust deficit, and anxiety after buying. Each is tagged desk research or assumption, so I always know which is which.

### 13 · Meet the three people I designed for  `03:41` · 28s · 55 words

I drafted six proto-personas, then merged them into three. Meet Ashna, the efficient shopper, who wants a genuine product at a fair price without losing her weekend.

**[step]** Abesh, the evidence seeker: "show me the benchmarks".

**[step]** And Krishna, the contributor, who shares honest reviews so others don't waste money. All three are assumptions I'd validate first.

### 14 · A bad buying journey, mapped  `04:09` · 26s · 54 words

To feel the problem, I made a journey map for Ashna buying headphones. Watch the line: curious, doubtful, exhausted when Reddit contradicts YouTube, a little hope, then regret when the price drops.

**[step]** Every low point is a pain point, and each became an opportunity: a shortlist, evidence first, trust signals, price history, and alerts.

### 15 · The problem, in one sentence  `04:35` · 20s · 47 words

That crystallised into a point of view, built with the five Ws. Who: buyers of high-value products. What: they need a trustworthy way to evaluate them. When: when money is at stake. Where: across Google, YouTube, Reddit and marketplaces. Why: affiliate bias, sponsored picks and fake reviews.

### 16 · Ten questions I wanted to answer  `04:55` · 22s · 52 words

Then I turned pain points into ten "How might we" questions. The first five: trust, fatigue, scattered evidence, long-term ownership, and real versus sponsored reviews.

**[step]** And five more: enough evidence, price anxiety, owners contributing, explainable recommendations, and a confident decision. Their numbers follow every idea and feature after, which kept scope honest.

## Ideate

### 17 · Ideate  `05:17` · 3s · 3 words

Stage three. Ideate.

### 18 · Lots and lots of ideas, no judging yet  `05:20` · 19s · 39 words

In ideation, quantity beats quality, and nothing gets criticised. So for every question I wrote ideas on sticky notes. About three hundred. This is one board: ideas for trust and source transparency. Messy and repetitive, and that's the point.

### 19 · Sorting the notes until patterns showed up  `05:39` · 45s · 103 words

Then the affinity diagram: grouping the sticky notes to find patterns. Eight themes came out, each with one key insight, and I grouped them into four product areas. First the trust layer: trust comes from transparency and evidence, not from asking people to blindly trust AI, and my principles came from here.

**[step]** The research layer: people want less unnecessary effort, not less information.

**[step]** The ownership layer: community knowledge only matters if it is trusted and kept up to date, and buyers care about years, not the first week.

**[step]** And the decision layer: the goal isn't to recommend products, it's to support confident decisions.

### 20 · Six rules every screen has to follow  `06:24` · 16s · 37 words

The clusters became six principles, which I used to judge every screen. Transparency over persuasion. Evidence over popularity. Confidence over conversion. Honest uncertainty. Empower, don't decide: no forced winners. And consistency. You'll see them settle real arguments.

### 21 · Picking what to build first  `06:40` · 22s · 53 words

Eight concepts, two weeks. So I ran a NUF test: score each idea on how New, Useful and Feasible it is, one to five, so fifteen is the top. Four concepts scored fourteen and became Version One. Ownership and decision support were Version Two. These are my own estimates, to revisit after interviews.

### 22 · Four ideas made the cut  `07:02` · 15s · 34 words

The four that scored fourteen became the heart of release one: evidence-backed research, a unified workspace, an AI decision assistant that explains trade-offs, and transparent AI. Each points back to the questions it answers.

## Prototype

### 23 · Prototype  `07:17` · 3s · 3 words

Stage four. Prototype.

### 24 · Where everything lives  `07:20` · 21s · 44 words

Structure first: the information architecture, an operational map of how the product works. Five tabs, and one shared destination, the Research Hub, reachable from everywhere so a buyer never loses their place. Click to zoom; this is the real diagram from my Figma file.

### 25 · Before and after: one buyer’s journey  `07:41` · 22s · 47 words

Here's the as-is flow beside the to-be flow. Today: four-plus platforms, anonymous opinions, a re-research loop, manual price checks, regret. With TyoKina: the same steps, in one place, with verified owners, a confidence check, price history, and alerts instead of rechecking. The steps stay; the friction goes.

### 26 · The paths through the app  `08:03` · 23s · 50 words

Then user flows: the path someone takes to finish a goal. I drew six task flows plus flowcharts. Here are two. The first is the research and confidence loop: search, review the evidence, compare if needed, and if you're not confident yet, keep researching. Buying isn't the only good ending.

### 27 · Pen and paper first  `08:26` · 23s · 49 words

Then wireframes, and I started on paper. This is my actual notebook: Home, Compare, Community and Profile, sketched by hand. Then the first digital version. Testing it against my principles changed three things: the rupee symbol, a fifth Saved tab, and a comparison that no longer picks a winner.

### 28 · What it should feel like  `08:49` · 28s · 62 words

Before hi-fi, a mood board. The question to answer: how should the user feel? For a high-stakes purchase, I wanted calm, clear and honest. I collected references for clean product cards, readable price charts, structured reviews and honest comparison tables. The takeaways: navy for calm authority, one accent for decisions and prices, numbers in a monospace face, and lots of white space.

### 29 · The design system  `09:17` · 24s · 52 words

The design system is deliberately small: fifteen colour tokens, no hard-coded colours. Navy for authority. One amber for every accent, and small text stays ink because amber is too light to read. Outfit for words, DM Mono only for numbers, so prices and scores are easy to compare. Fewer decisions, more confidence.

### 30 · Try it: the Research Hub, live  `09:41` · 48s · 82 words

This is the heart of the product, and it's the real app, running right here. Five questions a buyer asks. "Is it good?" An evidence score and a summary written only from cited sources.

**[step]** "Who says so?" Every source scored, affiliate links flagged.

**[step]** "Will it last?" Reliability, with an honest "limited evidence" label.

**[step]** "Is it priced right?" A chart you can flip between weekly and yearly, and alerts.

**[step]** And "What do owners say?" Verified owners, and a way to ask them questions.

### 31 · Compare without crowning a winner  `10:29` · 34s · 63 words

Compare is where my principles got tested. The rule: no winners. My first version starred the best in each row; I removed it. Now the strongest value gets a neutral mint pill. Your priorities float to the top. And the Decision check shows evidence coverage and open uncertainties, then lets you choose buy, wait or skip. Wait is a successful ending. Try it.

### 32 · Things I only found by building it  `11:03` · 26s · 53 words

Building it taught me things a mockup never would. Search should forgive typos. If we don't cover a product, say so. People lose their place, so the app tracks progress. A price drop should reopen your decision with a "what changed" note. And accessibility: focus, keyboard, forty-four pixel targets, from 320 pixels up.

### 33 · How it makes money without selling trust  `11:29` · 24s · 53 words

One last decision, a business one. If recommendations can be bought, the product dies. So revenue never changes what buyers see: no sponsored listings, no paid rankings. I removed them from my canvas because my own research says buyers distrust paid placement. Revenue: disclosed affiliate commissions, a premium tier, later anonymised insight reports.

## Test

### 34 · Test  `11:53` · 3s · 3 words

Stage five. Test.

### 35 · How I’ll find out if I’m wrong  `11:56` · 25s · 60 words

This is design validation, and I've been careful. Every key assumption has a method and a pass mark. Buyers spend many hours on one purchase: confirmed if four of six report five-plus hours. Showing sources raises trust: confirmed by an A/B test. Owners will contribute: confirmed if twenty percent start a review. Each can be proven wrong. That's the point.

### 36 · Five tasks for the first test  `12:21` · 17s · 39 words

And a usability round: five tasks, five recent buyers, think-aloud. Judge a summary. Compare two products. Decide on timing. Set an alert in under a minute. Update an old review. I'll measure success, ease, trust, and SUS, targeting seventy-plus.

## Reflect

### 37 · What I learned  `12:38` · 64s · 143 words

Four things I'd actually carry into the next project. One: a broad topic isn't a research question. I started with "how people buy things" and got lost; research only became useful once I wrote a goal and specific questions.

**[step]** Two: a persona only counts if it changes the design. Ashna's regret became price alerts, Abesh's doubts became the "Will it last?" tab, Krishna's wish to be heard became verified owners.

**[step]** Three: my own principles caught my own mistakes. I'd put a star on the best value in Compare, a "Buy now" button on the research page, and sponsored listings in my business canvas. Each broke a rule I'd written, so each came out.

**[step]** And four: working isn't the same as validated. A real prototype made it feel finished, but nobody except me has used it. So I wrote down how I'll find out.

### 38 · If I did it again  `13:42` · 21s · 49 words

If I did it again: interviews before personas, test the decision check early, and confirm Nepali price data is accessible. Next in the app: warranty and seller type, because dealer versus grey import matters in Nepal, marking uncited sentences, real credited photos, and dark mode. But first, the interviews.

### 39 · Thank you  `14:03` · 14s · 32 words

TyoKina turns twenty open tabs into one evidence-backed decision, and it's honest about what it still needs to prove. Thank you. I'd love your questions, and your help finding people to interview.

## If you run long

Cut, in this order: *Four themes* (12), *Concepts to features* (20), *Information architecture* (22), *Business model* (29). Each is about 20 to 25 seconds and none is needed for the story to land.

## If you run short

Linger on the *Research Hub* slide (26): step through all five questions and let the phone scroll. It is the most convincing minute of the talk.
